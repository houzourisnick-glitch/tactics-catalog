\#\# Foundational Framework

| Concept | Operational Meaning |  
|---------|---------------------|  
| Justice | Inquiry examines identity, equity, power, representation, voice, material conditions, and consequences. |  
| Inquiry | Students generate questions, investigate evidence, compare perspectives, construct knowledge, and revise claims. |  
| Action | Students communicate or apply learning to an authentic audience or meaningful response, with ethical limits. |  
| Critical literacy | Students examine how language, texts, images, institutions, and cultural narratives construct reality and distribute power. |  
| Counter-archive | A deliberately curated set of sources that challenges a single dominant account or expands whose knowledge counts. |  
| Provisional claim | A defensible working interpretation intentionally open to testing and revision. |  
| Ethical limit | A precise boundary describing what the evidence, interpretation, or action does not justify. |  
| Source tension | A meaningful relationship in which sources confirm, complicate, contradict, historicize, or reframe one another. |  
| Student agency | Students generate, select, interpret, revise, or communicate knowledge rather than merely reproduce teacher-provided conclusions. |  
| Epistemic authority | The socially or institutionally granted power to define what counts as knowledge, truth, evidence, or credibility. |

\#\# Core Inquiry Movement

A critical inquiry lesson or unit generally moves through the following sequence:

1\. Orient to a meaningful problem or tension.  
2\. Generate or refine an open-ended question.  
3\. Form a provisional claim.  
4\. Gather evidence from an anchor text or source.  
5\. Introduce a secondary source, counter-narrative, data point, or multimodal artifact.  
6\. Stress-test the claim through comparison, dialogue, contradiction, or source evaluation.  
7\. Revise or qualify the claim.  
8\. State an ethical or epistemic limit.  
9\. Construct an appropriate written, oral, multimodal, or public response.  
10\. Reflect and generate the next question.

The sequence may be compressed or expanded, but claim formation, source tension, revision, and an ethical limit must not disappear when they are central to the learning target.

\#\# Text-Set Design Patterns

| Pattern | Use |  
|---------|-----|  
| Triangulation | Three sources illuminate one problem from different positions, genres, or forms of knowledge. |  
| Counter-text | A source contests, complicates, or reframes the anchor text’s assumptions or conclusions. |  
| Historical layering | Sources from different periods reveal continuity and change without collapsing historical contexts. |  
| Institutional collision | Personal or literary testimony meets law, policy, data, or institutional language. |  
| Media contrast | Speech, image, narrative, data, and legal language construct meaning through different rhetorical systems. |  
| Student choice menu | Students choose a source that meets a shared inquiry goal, increasing agency and differentiation. |  
| Counter-archive | A curated collection expands whose knowledge counts or challenges a single dominant account. |  
| Quantitative context card | A concise, verified statistic or data display complicates a literary, historical, or rhetorical interpretation. |  
| Adaptation comparison | An adaptation changes medium, audience, emphasis, or representation and therefore becomes a source of interpretive tension. |  
| Witness and authority pairing | Testimony or lived experience is examined alongside an institutional account, with attention to credibility and power. |

For every multi-source set, explain:

\- What each source reveals.  
\- What each source cannot establish alone.  
\- How the sources relate.  
\- Which source or perspective holds institutional authority.  
\- Which voices, experiences, or forms of knowledge remain absent.

\#\# 3-Turn Claim Revision Model

Use this model whenever claim revision is a stated learning target.

\#\#\# Turn 1: Provisional claim and strongest evidence

Students or the teacher state an initial interpretation and identify the strongest evidence currently available.

\`\`\`text  
I’m entering this text with the assumption that \_\_\_\_\_\_.  
The strongest evidence I see so far is \_\_\_\_\_\_, which suggests \_\_\_\_\_\_.  
\`\`\`

\#\#\# Turn 2: Counter-evidence or counter-reading

Students or the teacher identify evidence, context, or an alternative interpretation that complicates the initial claim.

\`\`\`text  
A rigorous counterclaim or alternative reading posits that \_\_\_\_\_\_.  
Here’s the evidence that complicates my first claim: \_\_\_\_\_\_.  
\`\`\`

\#\#\# Turn 3: Revised claim and ethical or epistemic limit

Students or the teacher revise the initial interpretation and name its boundary.

\`\`\`text  
I am revising my initial claim because \_\_\_\_\_\_.  
A more defensible claim is \_\_\_\_\_\_.  
The ethical or epistemic limit of this claim is \_\_\_\_\_\_.  
\`\`\`

A claim revision must show a genuine change, qualification, narrowing, or increased complexity. Merely repeating the original claim with additional evidence does not constitute visible revision.

\#\# Language and Literacy

Use academic language as a bridge into disciplinary thinking. Vocabulary should be contextual, morphologically transparent where useful, bilingual where relevant, and immediately applied to evidence.

\#\#\# Core language frames

\- I notice… → I infer… → This positions the reader to…  
\- I’m entering this text with the assumption that…  
\- The strongest evidence warranting this claim is…  
\- This matters systemically because…  
\- A rigorous counterclaim posits that…  
\- I am revising my initial claim because…  
\- What remains uncertain is…  
\- This source reveals…, but it cannot establish…  
\- The author positions the audience to…  
\- The language constructs \_\_\_ as normal, natural, inevitable, or deviant.

\#\#\# Morphological analysis

When academic vocabulary is central to the lesson:

1\. Identify the meaningful morphemes.  
2\. Define the morphemes in accessible language.  
3\. Connect the word’s morphology to its disciplinary meaning.  
4\. Apply the word to a sentence from the text or source.  
5\. Ask students to use the word in a qualified claim.

Example:

\`\`\`text  
institutional \= institute \+ \-ion \+ \-al  
institute \= establish or organize  
\-ion \= process or result  
\-al \= relating to  
institutional \= relating to an organized system, authority, or institution  
\`\`\`

\#\#\# Syntax deconstruction

For a complex or rhetorically important sentence:

1\. Locate the independent clause.  
2\. Identify dependent clauses and modifying phrases.  
3\. Track pronouns and referents.  
4\. Identify modal verbs and degrees of certainty.  
5\. Identify active or passive voice.  
6\. Reconstruct the sentence in student-friendly language.  
7\. Analyze how the syntax positions the reader.

\#\#\# Modal and enthymeme analysis

When analyzing argument:

\- Identify modal verbs such as \*must\*, \*should\*, \*may\*, \*might\*, or \*cannot\*.  
\- Ask what level of certainty or obligation each modal creates.  
\- Identify the stated premise.  
\- Infer the unstated premise.  
\- Test whether the evidence warrants the conclusion.  
\- Name what the argument assumes but does not prove.

\#\# Critical Perspective Knowledge

Use critical perspectives as analytical tools, not predetermined conclusions.

\#\#\# Selection rules

\- Select one primary perspective by default.  
\- Use no more than one supporting perspective unless the task explicitly justifies additional layering.  
\- Define each selected perspective in accessible language.  
\- Provide guiding questions.  
\- Require specific textual, historical, visual, or multimodal evidence.  
\- State what the perspective may obscure.  
\- Match lens complexity to grade-level progression.  
\- Do not diagnose real people, authors, students, or communities.

\#\#\# Perspective prompts

| Perspective | Guiding Questions |  
|-------------|------------------|  
| Historical | What conditions shaped the text? What changes when we situate it in time? |  
| Institutional | What rules, policies, organizations, or authorities shape the situation? Who experiences the consequences? |  
| Cultural | What norms, identities, representations, and meanings are normalized or contested? |  
| Marxist / class | How do labor, ownership, material conditions, class relations, or ideology shape the text? |  
| Feminist / gender | How do gender, voice, representation, patriarchy, sexuality, or intersectionality shape power? |  
| Critical race | How are racial categories produced, maintained, challenged, or experienced systemically? |  
| Psychoanalytic | What desires, conflicts, repression, or identity tensions are represented without diagnosing real people? |  
| Postcolonial | How do empire, colonial power, othering, hybridity, voice, or decolonization shape the text? |  
| Poststructuralist / deconstructive | What binaries, silences, unstable meanings, or competing truth claims does the text produce? |

For every selected lens, state:

\`\`\`text  
Lens:  
Accessible definition:  
Guiding questions:  
Evidence required:  
What this lens reveals:  
What this lens may obscure:  
\`\`\`

\#\# Differentiation and Access

Differentiate access, not intellectual worth. Students may pursue the same conceptual goal through different supports, pathways, and forms of expression.

| Need or Barrier | Design Response |  
|-----------------|-----------------|  
| Decoding or fluency | Audio, chunking, shared reading, repeated reading, vocabulary frontloading. |  
| Language development | Bilingual tiles, home-language planning, translated labels, oral rehearsal, clarified syntax. |  
| Executive function | Checklists, visible sequences, claim/evidence templates, progress cards. |  
| Discourse access | Roles, rehearsal, sentence frames, written entry before public speaking. |  
| Advanced inquiry | Independent source choice, additional lens, counter-reading, source evaluation. |  
| Multimodal access | Images, audio, print, captions, visual maps, assistive technology. |  
| Text complexity | Chunked excerpts, glossaries, parallel texts, audio support, guided annotation. |  
| Writing production | Oral rehearsal, paragraph frames, speech-to-text, models, conferencing. |  
| Public participation risk | Private reflection, teacher-only submission, anonymous contribution, or alternative artifact. |  
| Technology or environment | Print equivalent, offline pathway, flexible grouping, reduced-distraction option. |

Do not:

\- Require students to disclose personal trauma or identity.  
\- Assign students to represent an entire group.  
\- Lower the conceptual target as the default response to access needs.  
\- Treat home language as a deficit.  
\- Confuse public speaking with intellectual participation.

\#\# Assessment Knowledge

A strong critical inquiry assessment measures both interpretation and inquiry behavior. It should show:

\- What students claim.  
\- What evidence they use.  
\- How they reason from evidence.  
\- How they respond to challenge.  
\- How their thinking changes.  
\- What they understand as uncertain or ethically limited.  
\- How they communicate to an intended audience.

| Assessment | Best Evidence |  
|------------|---------------|  
| Reading analysis | Annotation, evidence map, lens-based explanation. |  
| Argument writing | Micro-argument, seed essay, synthesis brief. |  
| Discussion | Move tracker, reflection card, teacher observation. |  
| Multimodal literacy | Observation/inference map, medium-specific analysis. |  
| Inquiry process | Question log, source rationale, revision history. |  
| Source evaluation | Verification record, authority analysis, source comparison. |  
| Claim revision | Provisional claim, counter-evidence, revised claim, ethical limit. |  
| Action | Audience-facing artifact, evidence record, feasibility analysis, reflection on impact and limits. |

\#\#\# Minimum micro-argument structure

When the task is argumentative, require:

1\. Debatable claim.  
2\. Specific textual, historical, visual, or data evidence.  
3\. Reasoning or warrant.  
4\. Counterclaim or complication.  
5\. Revised claim.  
6\. Ethical or epistemic limit.

\#\# UDL and Access Principle

Universal Design for Learning supports multiple means of engagement, representation, and action/expression. Apply UDL as barrier analysis:

1\. Identify the barrier.  
2\. Determine whether it arises from the material, task, language, environment, or product.  
3\. Provide a pathway that preserves the intellectual target.  
4\. Check whether the pathway actually improves access.  
5\. Retain evidence of the student’s disciplinary thinking.

\#\#\# Barrier categories

\- \*\*Material:\*\* Text complexity, density, genre unfamiliarity.  
\- \*\*Task:\*\* Unclear instructions, hidden steps, excessive cognitive load.  
\- \*\*Language:\*\* Academic vocabulary, complex syntax, untranslated terms.  
\- \*\*Environment:\*\* Noise, seating, technology access, time pressure.  
\- \*\*Product:\*\* Rigid format requirements or a single mode of expression.  
\- \*\*Participation:\*\* Public speaking expectations, identity risk, or discussion inequity.

\#\# Source Verification Knowledge

Treat all AI-generated quotations, statistics, dates, captions, archival details, and contextual claims as \*\*Unverified Suspect Statements\*\* until verified.

\#\#\# Source Verification Record

For every source used in teacher or student-facing materials, record:

\- Author or creator.  
\- Title.  
\- Original publication or creation date.  
\- Edition, translation, adaptation, or transcript.  
\- Genre or source type.  
\- URL, archive, database, or repository location.  
\- Access date.  
\- Verification status: \`verified\`, \`contested\`, or \`unverified\`.  
\- Student-facing use status: \`approved\`, \`teacher-notes-only\`, or \`excluded\`.  
\- Notes concerning uncertainty, disagreement, or limitations.

\#\#\# Source-use rules

\- Never invent quotations, statistics, dates, page numbers, captions, or archival details.  
\- Label paraphrases as paraphrases.  
\- Do not place unverified or contested quotations in student-facing materials.  
\- State what each source can and cannot establish.  
\- Distinguish primary source, secondary source, adaptation, translation, transcript, commentary, and teacher-created excerpt.  
\- Preserve enough metadata for another teacher to locate and verify the source.

\#\# Action Design

Action connects interpretation to an audience, purpose, or meaningful response. It must remain evidence-based, feasible, consensual, and ethically bounded.

\#\#\# Action design sequence

1\. Define the audience.  
2\. Define the purpose.  
3\. Identify the evidence required by that audience.  
4\. Select a realistic sphere of influence.  
5\. Consider consent, risk, privacy, power, and unintended consequences.  
6\. Select an appropriate genre or medium.  
7\. Produce the artifact.  
8\. Receive feedback.  
9\. Revise.  
10\. Reflect on impact, limits, and next steps.

\#\#\# Possible action forms

\- Public archive or exhibit.  
\- Curated digital dossier.  
\- Curriculum recommendation.  
\- Policy analysis or recommendation brief.  
\- Evidence-based letter or op-ed.  
\- Public information resource.  
\- Community-facing presentation with consent.  
\- Reflective action or changed practice.  
\- Teacher-reviewed counter-narrative or multimodal artifact.

Public action is not automatically superior to private or classroom-based action. Select the least risky form that still meets the intellectual and communicative purpose.

\#\#\# Action safeguards

\- Do not require students to disclose personal experiences.  
\- Do not publish student work without consent and local policy compliance.  
\- Do not include identifying information in public artifacts.  
\- Do not require students to participate in political advocacy.  
\- Provide a meaningful alternative response mode.  
\- Name foreseeable risks and unintended consequences.  
\- Distinguish evidence-based civic communication from unsupported persuasion.

\#\# AI-Specific Design Ethics

The AI curriculum engine is a design assistant, not an autonomous curriculum authority.

Treat AI outputs as \*\*Unverified Suspect Statements\*\* until checked. The engine must not:

\- Invent sources or quotations.  
\- Predetermine student conclusions.  
\- Replace student composition or analysis.  
\- Treat a critical lens as proof of one political answer.  
\- Require personal disclosure.  
\- Publish student work without consent.  
\- Substitute activity density for cognitive coherence.  
\- Use theoretical labels without accessible definitions and evidence-based application.

Teacher review remains necessary before classroom deployment.
